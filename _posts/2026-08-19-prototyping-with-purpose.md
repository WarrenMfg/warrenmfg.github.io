# Summary

- Define what the prototype must validate and map the assumptions it is meant to test (Benraouane & Harrington, 2021, p. 201).
- Build a rough end-to-end version first, then improve the weakest part as time permits (Ameisen, 2020, p. 45).
- Approve AI coding and prototyping tools with explicit policies on code review, data privacy, and repository access (Runtasewee & González Sánchez, 2026, p. 236).
- Confirm data access, quality, and permissions before prototyping begins.
- Schedule early integration checkpoints with innovation team members, since integration problems tend to surface late (Tidd, 2006, p. 293).
- Name a product owner who is available to make quick decisions (Runtasewee & González Sánchez, 2026, p. 161).
- Show stakeholders something they can see (Schweitzer & Tidd, 2018, p. 162).
- Hold the approval gate against agreed go/no-go rules (Tidd & Bessant, 2014, p. 182), and expect that cancellation or redirection is a real possibility (Kerzner, 2023, p. 202).

An approved proposal is a bet, not a result. The prototype is where that bet meets evidence. Done well, it answers a specific question quickly, gives non-technical stakeholders something real to react to, and leaves leadership with a clear decision. Done poorly, it becomes an open-ended build that nobody can evaluate. The sections below follow a rough order for innovation teams, from defining what the prototype must prove to deciding what happens next.

# Decide what the prototype must prove

A prototype is an early or incomplete model of a product, built to test a concept or process (Kerzner, 2023, p. 168). It also works as a tangible communication tool that shares information with everyone involved (Kerzner, 2023, p. 168). That second role matters for executives, because a prototype is as much a conversation device as a technical artifact.

Prototypes serve several purposes, including initial exploration of an idea, determining the appropriate direction, attracting funding, garnering feedback, defining patents, and facilitating the manufacturing process (Kerzner, 2023, p. 168). No single prototype should try to serve all of them. Choose the one or two that matter most for the decision ahead.

Validation gives the work its logic. It reduces the initial amount of uncertainty by letting a team stress test its assumptions (Benraouane & Harrington, 2021, p. 200). Those assumptions typically concern the customer, the product, the market, the timing, the competition, and more (Benraouane & Harrington, 2021, p. 201). One practical approach is to define what you need to validate, map your assumptions, and decide on a validation method such as experiments, user interviews, or lab testing (Benraouane & Harrington, 2021, p. 201).

The kind of prototype follows from the question. A proof-of-principle prototype verifies key functional aspects of a design but usually lacks the full functionality of the final product (Kerzner, 2023, p. 168). A visual prototype shows size and appearance but not functionality, and a paper prototype is a printed or hand-drawn representation of a software interface that supports early testing before costlier design effort (Kerzner, 2023, p. 169). A user experience prototype shows enough of the appearance and function of a product to be used for user research (Kerzner, 2023, p. 169).

A minimum viable product is the minimum configuration of an idea that can run a build, measure, and learn cycle, and its purpose is to generate data that helps adjust the core idea (Goller & Bessant, 2017, p. 247). In the validation stage, the minimum viable product is a limited version of the final product used primarily to engage the customer (Benraouane & Harrington, 2021, p. 201).

> Matching the prototype type to the question it must answer keeps the effort focused: a proof of principle suits technical risk, a visual or user experience prototype suits stakeholder reactions, and a minimum viable product suits the need for data from real use.

Agree on success and cancellation criteria before building starts, so the later approval decision rests on evidence rather than enthusiasm.

Watch for failure aversion, which can lead teams to pick an approach unlikely to fail even though it probably will not deliver what the customer wanted (Kerzner, 2023, p. 168).

# Build the smallest thing that can answer the question

Speed is the team's main advantage during prototyping, and rigor is the discipline that keeps speed useful. The two pull against each other, so the practices below are chosen to keep both.

Start with a rough version of the whole thing. A first end-to-end prototype is lackluster by design, because its goal is to have every piece of the pipeline in place so the team can prioritize which piece to improve next (Ameisen, 2020, p. 45).

> A full prototype is the easiest way to identify the bottleneck that limits impact (Ameisen, 2020, p. 45).

Rapid prototyping techniques follow the same logic by implementing part, but not all, of a design so the team can quickly and cheaply test the parts most likely to have problems (Kerzner, 2023, p. 170). The alternative is costly, because building the full design repeatedly is expensive and time-consuming (Kerzner, 2023, p. 170).

AI tools can compress this work considerably. AI coding assistants improve engineering productivity by reducing boilerplate work and accelerating feature development (Runtasewee & González Sánchez, 2026, p. 236). AI prototyping builders generate a working prototype from a prompt, which lowers the technical barrier to creating minimum viable products and demos (Runtasewee & González Sánchez, 2026, p. 236). AI tools can also quickly turn early-stage ideas into wireframes, presentation drafts, and web prototypes for stakeholders (Runtasewee & González Sánchez, 2026, p. 229).

The speed comes with conditions. Coding assistants require governance, including policies on code review, data privacy, proprietary repository access, and acceptable use (Runtasewee & González Sánchez, 2026, p. 236). They are accelerators, but they do not replace science, engineering, and development knowledge (Runtasewee & González Sánchez, 2026, p. 236). Tool choices also deserve early attention, because switching technical frameworks mid-project adds hidden costs, so framework choices should align with the team's existing expertise (Runtasewee & González Sánchez, 2026, p. 236).

Data access deserves the same early attention. Data availability is one of the risks a project lead should identify early, along with model performance and ethical concerns (Runtasewee & González Sánchez, 2026, p. 18). Identifying gaps in talent, tools, and infrastructure early is essential to avoid delays (Runtasewee & González Sánchez, 2026, p. 25). Mapping data sources to use cases confirms that data is available and suitable for modeling (Runtasewee & González Sánchez, 2026, p. 119). Quality matters as much as access, because data accuracy, completeness, and consistency directly dictate model performance (Runtasewee & González Sánchez, 2026, p. 119). Confirm access, quality, and permissions before the team starts building, not after.

Rigor, in a prototype, means honest comparison rather than polish. Locking in evaluation datasets and metrics supports fair comparisons between prototype iterations (Runtasewee & González Sánchez, 2026, p. 96). Leaders should also keep expectations realistic about what a prototype can show. Prototypes can reduce the risk that a design will not perform as intended, but they generally cannot eliminate all risk (Kerzner, 2023, p. 170). Because of differences in materials, processes, and fidelity, a prototype may fail where the production design would have been sound, or perform acceptably where the production design is flawed (Kerzner, 2023, p. 169).

> A prototype result is evidence, not proof.

Watch for the endless experimentation loop, which clear decision points, locked evaluation datasets and metrics, and risk-aware iteration limits help avoid (Runtasewee & González Sánchez, 2026, p. 95).

# Run the team like a short sprint

The team lead's job is to break the work into pieces people can own, keep those pieces moving, and make sure they fit together at the end.

Planning for innovation work is different from planning for routine work. Innovation projects may lack a detailed statement of work, so a work breakdown structure may begin with only high-level activities (Kerzner, 2023, p. 211). A practical response is to detail a block of foreseeable activities and expand the plan on a moving window as design, experimentation, and testing progress (Kerzner, 2023, p. 211). Plans must stay flexible, because the failure of a single test can force a major change in the work breakdown structure and the direction of the project (Kerzner, 2023, p. 211).

A Gantt chart remains a useful way to show delegation. It lists activities with bars for estimated duration, and columns can show who is responsible for each activity (White & Bruton, 2011, p. 188). It has limits, though: Gantt charts are hard to maintain when delays occur and do not show sequential dependencies among activities (White & Bruton, 2011, p. 188). Use it as a shared picture of ownership and timing, and expect to redraw it often.

A short-cycle rhythm keeps the team aligned. In Scrum, a sprint is a fixed-time cycle, typically between one and three weeks, in which part of the project is completed (Runtasewee & González Sánchez, 2026, p. 159). Modify this as necessary. A daily stand-up aligns the team on progress and blockers (Runtasewee & González Sánchez, 2026, p. 159). Often, innovation teams meet twice per day: once to align in the morning, and once to review status and blockers in the evening.

> Team dynamics deserve deliberate care when the schedule is tight.

A project lead reads day-to-day team dynamics, coaches team members, and works to understand blockers (Runtasewee & González Sánchez, 2026, p. 2). Psychological safety lets team members admit gaps and normalizes test, fail, and learn cycles (Runtasewee & González Sánchez, 2026, p. 92). Fear of ridicule or of the unknown can suppress contributions in innovation settings, so organizations must create a culture where people are not afraid to offer ideas, good or bad (Kerzner, 2023, p. 170). A team that hides problems to protect the schedule will hand leadership a prototype that looks finished and is not.

> The hardest part of delegation is putting the delegated work back together.

In studied software firms, the major problems of projects arose when the pieces were integrated, and these problems tended to appear late in production (Tidd, 2006, p. 293). Team members often worked largely in isolation on their own pieces, so communication became critical at the integration stage (Tidd, 2006, p. 293). Integration problems occurred when validation and cross-checking were insufficient, and part-time engineers made them worse (Tidd, 2006, p. 294). One firm improved integration by combining technical disciplines in product teams and passing integration feedback directly to designers early (Tidd, 2006, p. 294).

Watch for isolated work: schedule early integration checkpoints so the pieces meet well before the final demonstration.

# Close the loop with stakeholders

Feedback only helps if someone owns it and acts on it. A project lead builds feedback-to-response pipelines rather than feedback inboxes, maps feedback touchpoints across roles, and identifies who owns each type of loop (Runtasewee & González Sánchez, 2026, p. 92). A project lead can also celebrate loop closures in sprint demos (Runtasewee & González Sánchez, 2026, p. 92). At the end of a sprint the team demonstrates completed work to stakeholders to gather feedback (Runtasewee & González Sánchez, 2026, p. 159).

Short, repeated rounds produce visible gains. In one case, a team showed its minimum viable product to end users in rapid iterative testing rounds early and late in the week, incorporated feedback in between, and saw user satisfaction rise sharply in the second round (Kerzner, 2023, p. 44).

The product owner is the pivotal stakeholder in this loop, and the role is a demanding one. A product owner sits under pressure from both the business and the team (Kerzner, 2023, p. 44). Executives sponsoring a prototype can help by protecting that person's time and authority to decide.

Watch for an unavailable product owner: Scrum relies on a product owner who is engaged and available to make quick decisions, because without a strong one the team can lose focus (Runtasewee & González Sánchez, 2026, p. 161).

# Make the prototype land with non-technical stakeholders

A prototype that only engineers can appreciate will not win a leadership decision. The goal is to let non-technical stakeholders experience the idea rather than imagine it.

Visuals do much of this work. Visuals quickly convey size, aesthetic, and functional aspects that are lengthy to explain in words (Schweitzer & Tidd, 2018, p. 162). Software tools now allow fast, engaging, and cost-efficient virtual prototypes that improve decision quality and engender trust (Schweitzer & Tidd, 2018, p. 162). Words alone can mislead, as one service showed: physical prototypes were essential in testing a cabin-based airport service, because people associate many different things with the word "cabin" (Schweitzer & Tidd, 2018, p. 162).

> Fidelity changes how people react.

Participants shown slick designs tend to treat them as the final product, whereas rudimentary designs invite suggestions for change (Schweitzer & Tidd, 2018, p. 163). Prototype fidelity typically rises as work proceeds, from abstract concept prototypes toward closer representations of reality (Goller & Bessant, 2017, p. 223). Rough versions suit shaping the idea with stakeholders, and polished versions suit the approval conversation.

When the goal is to inspire, a different kind of prototype can help. A visiontype is a realistic-looking, high-fidelity prototype that is easy to create precisely because it is smoke and mirrors, and it is not constrained by what the team knows how to build (Cagan & Jones, 2021, p. 210). Slide presentations rarely inspire anyone (Cagan & Jones, 2021, p. 210). A short demonstration of a believable experience will often do more for sponsor confidence than another deck.

Executives also need to read a demonstration correctly. A pilot may look impressive, but scaling it can expose integration challenges, ethical dilemmas, or hidden costs, so executives need to understand the difference between a proof of concept and a production deployment (Runtasewee & González Sánchez, 2026, p. 86). The team lead should say plainly what the prototype proves and what it does not.

Watch for overselling: label which features are simulated, so a polished demonstration is not read as a finished product.

# Decide at the gate

The prototype exists to support a decision, so the decision process deserves as much design as the build. A structured development system with clear decision points and agreed go/no-go rules is considered more effective than ad hoc decisions (Tidd & Bessant, 2014, p. 182).

Innovation gates behave differently from the gates of routine projects. Cancellation is highly likely, redirection to new scope is possible, and changes to scope, schedule, and cost can be major (Kerzner, 2023, p. 202).

> Leaders should treat a cancellation or a redirection as a legitimate outcome of a well-run gate, because it means the prototype did its job before larger money was committed.

Readiness criteria make the review concrete. Readiness checklists can cover performance, fairness, interpretability, and reproducibility, with stakeholders convened for a go/no-go review before the project advances (Runtasewee & González Sánchez, 2026, p. 96). The review should also return to the assumptions the team set out to test (Benraouane & Harrington, 2021, p. 201). If the prototype validated them, the case for continuing is strong. If it did not, the team has learned something valuable at a modest cost.

Watch for scope creep: plans that are too vague can lead to scope creep and poor accountability (Runtasewee & González Sánchez, 2026, p. 26). Seeking formal approval of key aspects such as technical approaches, timelines, and resources helps avoid scope creep (Runtasewee & González Sánchez, 2026, p. 20).
