> **About the reference article**
>
> **Title:** AI Assistance Reduces Persistence and Hurts Independent Performance
>
> **Link:** [https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.04721](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.04721){:target="\_blank" rel="noopener"}
>
> **Brief summary:** Across three randomized controlled trials with 1,222 participants, people who solved fraction or reading comprehension problems with an AI assistant did better while it was available, but did worse and gave up more often once it was taken away (Liu et al., 2026). The authors conclude that AI systems optimized for instant, complete answers may erode the persistence and skills they are meant to support, and that AI development should also prioritize long-term competence (Liu et al., 2026).

# Summary

- Treat persistence and unassisted skill as capabilities your organization must protect, and add them to your innovation capability reviews.
- Require every AI product team to define one guardrail metric for user capability, such as unassisted task success, next to its engagement and satisfaction metrics.
- Run randomized experiments that include an "AI removed" test phase before you scale any assistant that completes tasks for people.
- Design assistants that offer hints and explanations by default, and reserve direct answers for cases where speed matters more than learning.
- Keep people in the loop on judgment-heavy work, and rotate staff through unassisted practice on skills your firm cannot afford to lose.
- Set learning goals, not only output goals, for teams doing creative or exploratory work with AI.
- Watch for feedback loops in which AI output becomes the training data for the next generation of your product.

# A short experiment with a long shadow

Most debates about AI and work focus on what the technology can do. A recent paper asks a different question: what does it do to the person using it? The researchers ran three randomized trials, two on fraction arithmetic and one on reading comprehension, with 1,222 participants in total (Liu et al., 2026). In each trial, one group worked with an AI assistant for most of the problems, while a control group worked alone. Then the assistant was removed without warning, and everyone solved a final set of problems unaided.

The assistant was built to be maximally convenient. It already had each problem and its solution, so a participant could type a single word and receive a complete answer (Liu et al., 2026). That convenience helped while it lasted. After removal, however, participants in the first experiment solved fewer test problems than the control group and skipped more of them (Liu et al., 2026). In that experiment, the AI group solved 57 percent of the test problems versus 73 percent for the control group, and it skipped 20 percent versus 11 percent (Liu et al., 2026). The reading comprehension trial showed the same pattern, with a lower solve rate and a higher skip rate on the final problems (Liu et al., 2026).

The results were not uniformly clean. In the second experiment, the solve-rate gap held, but the difference in skip rate was not statistically significant in the aggregate analysis (Liu et al., 2026). The authors then looked at how people used the assistant. A majority of the AI group reported using it mainly to get answers directly, and that group showed the lowest test solve rate and the highest skip rate (Liu et al., 2026). Participants who used it for hints or clarifications did not show the same decline, although this comparison is cross-sectional and not necessarily causal (Liu et al., 2026).

The striking feature is the dose. These effects appeared after sessions of roughly 10 to 15 minutes (Liu et al., 2026).

> Fraction problems are far from strategy or AI product design, but the finding raises a management question worth asking before AI assistants become the default way every employee starts every task.

# Why persistence is an innovation capability

Skipping a problem is a small act of giving up. For innovation managers, giving up is not a small matter. Innovation work repeatedly runs into moments that feel hard, and people tend to lower their expectations about what perseverance can achieve when creative challenges start to feel difficult (Goller & Bessant, 2017, p. 88).

Willpower provides the ability to persist through the iterations needed to build a good idea and to endure the frustration that accompanies them (Goller & Bessant, 2017, p. 88). Innovation is also experimentation, so failure is frequent, and the skill lies in treating each failure as a learning opportunity while keeping enough resilience to continue (Goller & Bessant, 2017, p. 88).

> Persistence, in other words, is not a personality garnish. It is part of the machinery that turns uncertain ideas into working products.

The study's authors offer a mechanism for why assistance might erode that machinery. They propose that AI conditions people to expect immediate answers, which denies them the experience of working through challenges on their own (Liu et al., 2026). If that is right, an assistant that shortens the struggle also shortens the practice that builds the tolerance for struggle.

This matters most for the kind of work executives most want to accelerate. Exploratory work, such as framing a new market or debugging an unfamiliar technical problem, is where the first answer is rarely the right one. An organization that leans on an instant-answer assistant for that work may be trading short-term throughput for a weaker innovation muscle.

# Learning goals and the cost of skipping the struggle

Innovation research offers a useful lens here. With a learning goal orientation, failure is read as useful feedback, and it supports persistence (Goller & Bessant, 2017, p. 118). For complex and creative tasks, learning goals outperform performance goals (Goller & Bessant, 2017, p. 118). A performance goal asks whether the output met a target. A learning goal asks whether the person or team got better at producing it.

> An answer-first assistant quietly pushes users toward performance goals. It makes the output easy and the process optional.

Teams that adopt it without countervailing norms will therefore tend to be measured, and to measure themselves, by what they produced and not by what they learned. Learning goals need to be set on purpose to offset that pull.

The deeper point concerns how organizations build capability. Failure builds capability because the ability to manage innovation comes from trial and error followed by reflection and revision (Bessant & Tidd, 2015, p. 487). A tool that removes the trial and error also removes some of the raw material for reflection.

The same logic applies at the level of the firm. Absorptive capacity, the ability to absorb new knowledge, is a key factor in developing innovation management capability, and it is essentially about learning to learn (Tidd & Bessant, 2014, p. 137).

> If staff stop practicing the underlying skills, the firm's ability to recognize, assimilate, and apply new knowledge may weaken, even as its output rises.

That is the uncomfortable possibility the study points toward: **a company can look more productive while becoming less able to learn**.

# Capability decay is a strategic risk

Technology management literature has a name for part of this problem. Continuous reliance on AI systems can undermine users' critical thinking skills, because people who stop questioning AI results and stop analyzing independently lose some of their ability to make informed decisions (Urbach & Feulner, 2026, p. 257). The remedies include transparency, critical thinking training, and the habit of independently verifying AI recommendations (Urbach & Feulner, 2026, p. 257).

There is also a competitive dimension. A carmaker that moves design work to AI to save cost may find that its design data degrades and becomes outdated over time, because fewer humans are generating new ideas and insights, which risks making the company uncompetitive (Bala et al., 2026, p. 141). The lesson generalizes. AI systems depend on fresh human insight, and human insight depends on people who keep practicing their craft. Replace the practice, and you may eventually starve the system you replaced it with.

The study's authors are careful about how far their findings reach. They caution that the tasks were simple and that the cumulative effects of daily AI use over months or years remain unknown (Liu et al., 2026).

> Executives should not read the paper as proof that AI makes teams worse.

They should **read it as a reason to treat skill retention as a portfolio decision**: decide which capabilities are core to your advantage, and protect practice on those, while letting the rest be automated freely.

# Measure what the product does to the user

If you build AI products, the study raises an uncomfortable question about your scorecard. Product metrics are the ones that matter, since they represent the goals of the product, and projects often add guardrail metrics that should not decline below a given point (Ameisen, 2020, p. 25). One example of a usefulness metric is the proportion of times that users follow suggestions (Ameisen, 2020, p. 25).

A metric like that captures adoption, but it does not show whether users are becoming more or less capable. An assistant could score very well on acceptance while users slowly lose the ability to do the task without it. The study's authors argue that AI systems optimized only for short-term helpfulness risk eroding the human capabilities they are meant to support (Liu et al., 2026).

The practical response is to add a capability guardrail.

> A periodic unassisted task, such as a quick check that users can still perform the core job without the assistant, would turn the study's concern into a number that product teams can track.

For a writing assistant it might be an unaided first draft. For a coding assistant it might be a small debugging exercise. For an analytics copilot it might be interpreting a chart without suggestions.

Not every task needs protecting, so the skill is deciding which ones do. It also helps to avoid perfectionism. Teams should set a "good enough" threshold for model quality and stay focused on business value rather than marginal model gains (Runtasewee & González Sánchez, 2026, p. 252).

> A capability guardrail belongs in that same discipline, as one of the few numbers that say whether the product is making its users stronger or weaker.

# Design assistants that scaffold instead of substitute

The most useful finding for designers may be the contrast between usage patterns. Users who took direct answers fared worst, while users who asked for hints or clarifications did not show the same decline (Liu et al., 2026). The authors treat that pattern as tentative, and they describe the question of whether a Socratic assistant could preserve the benefits while avoiding the persistence costs as open (Liu et al., 2026). Even so, it suggests a design direction worth testing.

Product teams already make related decisions about the role of humans. A team must decide whether AI provides background support to humans, makes decisions directly, or both (Huyen, 2025, p. 31). Microsoft's Crawl-Walk-Run framework gradually raises automation, starting with mandatory human involvement and moving toward direct AI interaction with external users (Huyen, 2025, p. 31). The role of humans can change over time as the quality of the AI system improves (Huyen, 2025, p. 31). The same staged thinking can apply to the user's own skill: begin with strong scaffolding, and fade it as the person gains competence.

Human-in-the-loop mechanisms matter for a related reason. They improve accuracy and accountability in complex or high-risk scenarios where human judgment remains essential (Runtasewee & González Sánchez, 2026, p. 252).

> Keeping the human involved is not only a safety feature. It also keeps the human practicing.

Concretely, designers can try hint-first modes, "try first" prompts that ask users to attempt a step before the assistant responds, and practice modes that fade assistance as users improve. Direct answers can remain available, but as a deliberate choice and not the default path.

Designers should also watch for a second-order effect. Degenerate feedback loops arise when a system's outputs help generate its future inputs, which in turn influence its future outputs (Huyen, 2022, p. 233). Products that learn from user behavior should be reviewed for loops in which a dependent user base narrows the data the next model sees.

> If users stop contributing independent judgment, the signal the model learns from may thin out.

# Run the experiment your own product needs

The study's design is itself a lesson for product teams. Large digital firms formalize changes as hypotheses and test each as a randomized control trial in which one random sample of users is exposed to the change and another experiences business as usual (Iansiti & Lakhani, 2020, p. 133). Comparing the two groups shows whether the change actually affects the outcome instead of being spuriously correlated with it (Iansiti & Lakhani, 2020, p. 133).

Most product experiments stop at the moment of use. They compare task completion, satisfaction, or time saved while the assistant is present. The study adds a step many such experiments skip: after the learning phase, the assistant is removed and both groups face the same unassisted test problems (Liu et al., 2026). The authors also took care with the interface. Control participants in the later experiments saw a sidebar that was removed at test time, so that both groups experienced the same interface change (Liu et al., 2026).

Product teams can borrow the design directly.

> Before scaling an assistant that completes tasks for users, add a holdout group and an "AI off" probe to the pilot. Give both groups a short unassisted task at the end, and compare solve rates and give-up rates.

If the assisted group performs as well as the holdout, you have evidence that the assistant is not eroding skill on that task. If it performs worse, you have an early warning, and you can test a hint-first variant against the answer-first version before the pattern becomes habit.

# What the study cannot tell you

A fair reading requires attention to the limits. The experiments captured only brief exposure, and whether the effects accumulate or fade with sustained daily use is an open question (Liu et al., 2026). Participants were tested immediately after the assistant was removed, so the durability of the effects is unknown (Liu et al., 2026). The assistant was maximally helpful and gave complete answers on demand, so the results do not show how a more Socratic assistant would perform (Liu et al., 2026).

There is one more caution. The tasks were fraction arithmetic and reading comprehension, which are not the knowledge work most executives manage.

> The results should therefore shape hypotheses and pilot designs, not final policy.

A leader who bans assistants on the strength of this study would be overreacting, and a leader who ignores it would be underreacting. The sensible path is to test the effect in your own context, on your own tasks, with your own people.

# What to do next

Start small and specific. Pick two or three skills that are core to your competitive advantage and define how you will check that people can still perform them unassisted. Add a capability guardrail metric to the scorecard of any AI product or internal assistant. Pilot hint-first designs against answer-first designs, with an AI-off test at the end of each pilot.

Finally, build learning into the way teams work with AI. Well-managed post-project reviews that aim to learn and capture lessons rather than apportion blame are among the most important tools for improving innovation management (Bessant & Tidd, 2015, p. 487).

> Teams adopting AI should hold such reviews and ask how they reached a result, not only what they produced.

The central idea is simple. **The best collaborators help people finish the task and grow the skill at the same time.** AI products and AI-enabled organizations can aim for both, but only if someone decides to measure both.
