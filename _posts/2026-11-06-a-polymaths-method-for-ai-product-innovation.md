# Summary

- Require every discovery brief to name the problem at a higher level of abstraction before anyone proposes solutions, so the team can see which other fields have already solved the same problem.
- Ask teams for several analogies from fields that look unrelated to your product, not one analogy from a close competitor.
- Replace the single "most similar" comparison in project forecasts with a reference class of structurally similar projects, and check your internal estimate against it before committing budget.
- Staff the most uncertain AI bets with people who have worked across several domains, and stop screening them out with narrow hiring rubrics.
- Put at least one outsider from a different discipline or industry into every strategy and discovery session.
- Build and maintain a shared library of cross-domain cases, and schedule regular time to scan other industries.
- Use generative AI to widen the pool of analogies and always have a person check the output.
- Treat borrowing as a repeatable routine with an owner, a cadence, and a review of what was borrowed and what happened.

# Why the nearest idea is rarely the best one

Most AI product teams do their searching close to home. They study the competitor's roadmap, the last launch, and the model that worked on the previous project. That habit is efficient when problems repeat and costly when they do not. Novel product bets, new model capabilities, and unfamiliar markets belong to the second kind. The polymath's advantage is a different search habit: carry a pattern from a field that looks unrelated, check whether the structure underneath matches, and adapt the solution. This post lays out the evidence for that habit, explains why distance helps, and shows how to make borrowing a routine.

# Why carrying patterns across domains works

Leonardo da Vinci noted analogies between light and sound, between the branches of trees, rivers, and arteries, and between flying and swimming (Burke, 2020, p. 43). His range was a search method as much as a temperament. Other accounts point the same way. Louis Pasteur's discovery of the microbe depended on a background in crystallography, which helped him see why a microscopic lens was needed (Ahmed, 2018, p. 207). Psychologist Robert Root-Bernstein holds that polymaths contributed to their disciplines because of, not in spite of, their broad interests (Ahmed, 2018, p. 207). Historian Minor Myers found that the more diverse the knowledge and skills an individual could integrate, the more novel and useful combinations resulted (Ahmed, 2018, p. 209). Cognitive psychologist Rand Spiro makes the point from the other direction: creativity requires ideas, analogies, patterns, and perspectives from outside the domain being worked on (Ahmed, 2018, p. 216).

Management research reaches a similar conclusion. Innovation is often triggered less by advances at the frontier of knowledge than by applying existing knowledge in a new context, and firms without routines for bridging between small worlds of knowledge are at a disadvantage (Bessant & Venables, 2008, p. 205). Innovations are often combinations of ideas or knowledge from different sectors, as when the iPhone connected computing with telecommunications (van den Ende, 2021, p. 7).

> Breakthroughs often come from knowledge that already exists, applied somewhere new.

AI practitioners already work this way inside their models. Transfer learning moves knowledge gained on one task to speed learning on a new, related task (Huyen, 2025, p. 308). Google’s multilingual translation system translated directly between Portuguese and Spanish even though the training data had no Portuguese-Spanish examples (Huyen, 2025, p. 308). For large language models, knowledge gained from pre-training on text completion transfers to specialized tasks such as legal question answering and text-to-SQL (Huyen, 2025, p. 308). Product teams can apply the same logic to their own thinking, which is where the question of distance comes in.

# Why more distant analogies tend to help more

The analogies that come to mind on their own are the nearby ones. They work when problems repeat, but for novel problems people need to be reminded of things that are only abstractly or relationally similar, and the more creative the goal, the more that matters (Epstein, 2019, p. 97).

Experiments show the size of the effect. In Duncker’s radiation problem, a doctor must destroy a tumor with rays that are too weak to work at low intensity and too strong for the healthy tissue around the tumor at high intensity. The solution is to aim several weak rays at the tumor from different directions so they converge on it. Only about 10 percent of people solved it unaided, about 30 percent solved it after a story about a general converging on a fortress, and about half solved it after two stories from unrelated domains (Epstein, 2019, p. 99). A single analogy from a different domain tripled the share of solvers, and two analogies from disparate domains gave an even bigger boost (Epstein, 2019, p. 99). Most solvers did not notice the relevance of the fortress story until they were told to use it, which shows that people rarely borrow without a prompt (Epstein, 2019, p. 99).

Strategy work shows the same pattern. In a study of 150 business students writing strategies for a struggling computer mouse company, students given analogies produced more strategies, multiple analogies beat a single one, and more distant analogies were better for idea generation (Epstein, 2019, p. 104). Students pointed to Nike and McDonald's generated more strategic options than students pointed to Apple and Dell (Epstein, 2019, p. 105). The same students believed the best approach was to use a single example from the same field, so intuition runs against what works (Epstein, 2019, p. 105).

> Left alone, teams reach for the closest analogy. The better one is usually farther away.

Distant analogies also correct forecasts. Relying on a single, similar analogy keeps people in the inside view, where judgment rests on the details of the project in front of them (Epstein, 2019, p. 100). When private equity investors compared their own project with conceptually similar ones, they estimated their own return at about 50 percent above the comparison projects, and many then cut their initial estimates (Epstein, 2019, p. 102). Infrastructure projects worldwide that go over budget do so by an average of 28 percent, in part because managers focus on the details of their project and become overly optimistic (Epstein, 2019, p. 103). After an outside team compared a Scottish tram project with structurally similar projects and judged its £320 million estimate far too low, the tram opened three years late and was headed toward £1 billion (Epstein, 2019, p. 103).

The lesson is to use a set of analogies, not one. In a movie forecasting experiment, a full reference class of analogies predicted far better than the single best analogy, whose predictive power collapsed on its own (Epstein, 2019, p. 104). Netflix found that analogizing a customer to many customers with similar viewing histories worked better than decoding the traits of each movie (Epstein, 2019, p. 104). AI roadmaps are full of confident internal estimates, and a reference class is the cheapest check on them.

Distance is not the same as randomness. The analogies that matter share deep structure with the problem, and successful problem solvers first identify that deep structure before matching a strategy to it (Epstein, 2019, p. 107). In a sorting task built to test this skill, students who had taken classes in a range of domains were particularly good at finding common deep structures (Epstein, 2019, p. 106).

> Distance helps only when the structure underneath is the same.

Companies do this on purpose. Henkel, a multinational consumer goods company with businesses in laundry and home care, beauty care, and adhesives, is known for its many cross-industry innovation collaborations (Tidd, 2014, p. 91). It deliberately favors a certain distance between its own methods and those of cross-industry partners because that difference fosters radical innovations, and it accepts the added risk (Tidd, 2014, p. 92). Solutions transferred across industries by analogy may be technical, such as patents or technologies, or market-related, such as new forms of usage and new markets, and BMW's iDrive steering device is based on established joystick technology from the computer industry (Tidd, 2014, p. 113).

# Why teams with range find more

The same logic explains why mixed teams outperform uniform ones when the path is unclear. In the labs that Dunbar observed, the labs whose scientists had more diverse professional backgrounds offered more and more varied analogies (Epstein, 2019, p. 109). For relatively straightforward challenges, those labs started with analogies to other, very similar experiments (Epstein, 2019, p. 109). In some lab meetings a new analogy entered the conversation every four minutes on average, some of them from outside biology entirely (Epstein, 2019, p. 110). When two labs hit the same filter problem, the mixed-background lab solved it in the meeting through an analogy from a member with a medical degree, while the lab of E. coli experts spent weeks experimenting (Epstein, 2019, p. 110).

Patent data point the same way. As ambiguity and uncertainty increase, breadth becomes increasingly important, while on well-defined problems specialists work very well (Epstein, 2019, p. 185). An analysis of fifteen years of patents from 32,000 teams found that in high-uncertainty domains, teams including members with experience across a wide variety of technologies were more likely to make a splash (Epstein, 2019, p. 185). In comic books, breadth of genre experience, not years of experience, separated more innovative creators, and an individual who had worked in four or more genres was more innovative than a team with the same collective breadth (Epstein, 2019, p. 187).

> As uncertainty rises, so does the value of breadth.

Serial innovators are adept at using analogous domains for finding inputs to invention and tend to read more, and more broadly, than other technologists (Epstein, 2019, p. 189). Mechanistic hiring practices can screen out high-potential innovators whose wide-ranging interests do not fit a narrow rubric (Epstein, 2019, p. 190). Leaders who hire for the narrowest fit in an AI organization may be filtering out the people best placed to bring in the next idea.

# Creativity techniques for discovery and strategy sessions

Several established techniques turn the evidence above into session formats.

**Climb the ladder of abstraction.** Restating the problem in higher-level language lets a team see the same problem being solved in different contexts (Goller & Bessant, 2017, p. 32). Henry Ford's team saw the moving assembly line in how workers at a meat-packing plant disassembled carcasses along an overhead conveyor and reversed the process (Goller & Bessant, 2017, p. 30). McDonald's was a powerful influence on the development of the Aravind Eye Clinics in India, which bring low-cost eye surgery to the masses (Tidd & Bessant, 2018, p. 65). The Aravind system brought the average cost of a cataract operation down to $25 (Goller & Bessant, 2017, p. 31). Turnaround-time problems in airlines, hospitals, and Formula 1 pit stops share a common structure, and solutions such as single-minute die exchange moved between them (Goller & Bessant, 2017, p. 31). In a product session, the equivalent move is to restate "improve model onboarding" as "get a newcomer to competence quickly" and ask who else solves that.

**Copy, adapt, paste.** Ramon Vullings summarizes cross-industry borrowing as "copy, adapt, paste" and compares the practitioner to an idea DJ who samples from one world and mixes into another (Goller & Bessant, 2017, p. 32).

**Force a random connection.** Synectics aims to make the familiar strange and the strange familiar, using metaphor, analogy, and simulation to produce new associations (Bessant & Tidd, 2015, p. 141). Random juxtaposition takes two random elements and forces a relationship between them to open new pathways (Bessant & Tidd, 2015, p. 142).

> Ask for five analogies from five unrelated fields, then test which one shares the problem's structure.

**Match problems to solutions by pattern.** TRIZ grew from reviewing thousands of patents and finding that solutions across widely different fields shared underlying patterns, which Altshuller reduced to 40 principles of invention (Goller & Bessant, 2017, p. 33). Companies now use patent libraries to match problems and solutions by abstracting them to a generic level (Goller & Bessant, 2017, p. 33). Nature is another source: barbed wire was inspired by thorny hedges, an example of biomimicry (van den Ende, 2021, p. 30).

**Invite people who do not share your frame.** Creativity workshops with heterogeneous participants stimulate the forced fitting of unrelated issues and the exchange of divergent knowledge (Schweitzer & Tidd, 2018, p. 85). ISO 56002 suggests mixing disciplines, professional backgrounds, and cultural backgrounds in innovation teams, and cognitive diversity supports divergent thinking (Benraouane & Harrington, 2021, p. 141). Run the session with care, because brainstorming has shown detrimental effects on the quantity and quality of ideas, and enforcing its rules, brainwriting, and incubation time help (Goller & Bessant, 2017, p. 160).

**Use generative AI as an analogy engine.** Generative AI tools can act as accelerators for ideation in AI projects (Runtasewee & González Sánchez, 2026, p. 228-229). Ask a model to restate the problem abstractly and propose how five unrelated industries solve it, then have the team judge which ones match the deep structure. The probabilistic nature of foundation models makes them useful for creative tasks, but the same nature causes inconsistency and hallucination (Huyen, 2025, p. 105). Treat every analogy the model offers as a hypothesis for people to test, not an answer.

# Building cross-domain borrowing into the innovation process

One good session changes an afternoon. A routine changes an organization. Routines are patterns of behavior that an organization learns and practices, reinforces, and embeds in the way it does things, and the key point about them is that they are learned (Tidd & Bessant, 2014, p. 278). Organizations develop and refine the tools they use to search for innovation opportunities, and these search tools are themselves routines (Bessant & Tidd, 2015, p. 223).

The case for deliberate routines is stronger because the default pulls inward. Searching harder across existing networks can be the worst thing to do, because the ties that bind become the ties that blind, which makes weak ties valuable for radical innovation (Bessant & Venables, 2008, p. 205). In discontinuous innovation, effective search relies on peripheral vision, weak signals, and links with a heterogeneous population (Tidd & Bessant, 2018, p. 120).

> Borrowing becomes a capability only when it has an owner, a cadence, and a review.

Other organizations show what the routines look like. Henkel visits leading trade fairs in other industries, such as food and packaging, and so identifies trends there at an early stage that may later matter in its own sector (Tidd, 2014, p. 92). Boston Consulting Group built an intranet of exhibits sorted by discipline, concept, and strategic theme so consultants could reason by wide-ranging analogy (Epstein, 2019, p. 105). After the Scottish tram overrun, other UK infrastructure projects began requiring managers to make analogies to many outside projects of the past (Epstein, 2019, p. 103). Individuals can train openness by overcoming their routines and feeding their minds with diverse inputs, since a bigger bag of experiences allows more varied connections (Goller & Bessant, 2017, p. 79).

A workable routine for an AI product organization has five parts:

- **A standing slot.** Reserve twenty minutes in every discovery review to name the problem at a higher level of abstraction and list three analogies from unrelated fields.
- **An owner.** Assign one person to curate a shared library of cross-domain cases, including what was borrowed, from where, and what happened.
- **A rotating outsider.** Invite someone from another discipline or industry to each strategy session, and rotate the seat.
- **A forecast check.** Before approving any estimate, compare it with a reference class of structurally similar projects from other domains.
- **A quarterly review.** Look back at which borrowed ideas shipped, which failed, and which fields produced the best matches, and adjust where the team searches next.

Each part is small. Together they turn a polymath's habit of mind into something an organization can practice, measure, and improve.
