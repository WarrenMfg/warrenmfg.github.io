# Summary

- Put people with range in the seats that make the hardest AI calls: for novel, ambiguous problems, favor leads who bridge disciplines over single-domain experts, and judge them on how well they update after surprises.
- Rewrite hiring rubrics and job slots that screen out wide-ranging candidates, and look for varied avocations and a habit of focusing on the interfaces between systems.
- Send problems that have stalled inside your organization to people far from the field, because distance from the specialty often helps.
- Form small cross-disciplinary teams for high-uncertainty work, and rotate people across teams so the same collaborators do not keep working together.
- Hire and promote AI engineers who span product, data, and models rather than splitting those skills into separate roles.
- Protect bridging work from short-term evaluation, because it tends to be slow to earn recognition and to pay off later.

# Where durable advantage comes from

Every leader building with AI is asking the same question: when so much routine work can be assisted or automated, where does lasting advantage come from? One tempting answer is more depth, meaning deeper experts, narrower roles, and sharper specialization. This post makes the complementary case.

> In novel and ambiguous territory, which is where innovation lives, people who move between fields tend to see what specialists miss.

History, evidence from open innovation, and the early shape of AI engineering all point the same way, and they carry practical lessons for how you hire, staff, and lead.

# Polymaths keep returning when knowledge outgrows specialists

Breadth is not a modern fad. In classical antiquity, traditional China, the Islamic world, and the Western Middle Ages, a number of individuals were admired for unusual breadth of knowledge, and a few were already criticized for lacking depth (Burke, 2020, p. 10). Aristotle is the archetype: he contributed to almost every field of human knowledge then in existence and founded many new fields (Ahmed, 2018, p. 68).

What do such people actually contribute?

> The distinctive contribution of polymaths is to see connections between fields that have been separated and to notice what specialists inside a discipline have failed to see (Burke, 2020, p. 5).

That is an innovation capability, not a personality trait. It is the ability to carry a pattern from a place where it is ordinary to a place where it is new.

> Polymaths also tend to appear at frontiers.

New disciplines offer polymaths a role because their first generation of teachers was trained in something else, but the opportunity comes only once, since the second generation is trained inside the new discipline (Burke, 2020, p. 151). AI engineering is a new term that evolved out of machine learning engineering (Huyen, 2025, p. 48). Leaders who treat it as a settled specialty with settled career ladders may be closing the window early.

# Every knowledge explosion pushed the world toward specialization

Polymaths became rare not because curiosity faded but because knowledge outgrew people. By the seventeenth century, complaints that there were too many books multiplied, and printing, once viewed as a solution to scarce information, had become a problem itself (Burke, 2020, p. 77).

> The principal response to the explosion of knowledge was to specialize, which reduced the amount of information any one person needed to master (Burke, 2020, p. 130).

Specialization was rational for individuals. It helped scholars keep their heads above water in the flood of information, and for ambitious academics, creating new specialties was also a way to stand out (Burke, 2020, p. 141). Organizations followed the same logic when they carved work into narrow functions and narrow job descriptions.

The costs accumulated, though. Specialization was sooner or later perceived as a problem in itself, which gave rise to a movement aimed at restoring the lost unity of knowledge (Burke, 2020, p. 141). The explosion of knowledge has made it impossible for all but a few dedicated individuals to keep up with even a few disciplines, which prompted many collective attempts to solve the problem (Burke, 2020, p. 214).

One consequence is that breadth has migrated from people to structures. As knowledge has expanded, diversity and interdisciplinarity have become a team or organizational consideration rather than an individual one (Ahmed, 2018, p. 238). Generative AI intensifies the pattern, because the volume of what any leader should understand keeps growing. The practical question is how to build breadth into teams while still valuing the rare individual who has it.

# Why distance from the specialty pays

The sharpest evidence for breadth comes from open innovation, where organizations post their hardest problems to outsiders. NASA spent three decades unable to predict solar particle storms, posted the problem on an open innovation platform in 2009, and within six months a retired engineer solved it using radio waves picked up by telescopes (Epstein, 2019, p. 157). That engineer had worked with specialist teams and found that they often got mired in small details at the expense of practical solutions (Epstein, 2019, p. 157).

This was not a fluke.

> A Harvard researcher found that the further a problem was from a solver's own expertise, the more likely the solver was to solve it (Epstein, 2019, p. 160).

One reason is that as specialists become more narrowly focused, the box around their thinking becomes like nesting dolls, and even specialists who escape one box may be stuck inside the next (Epstein, 2019, p. 159). Increasing specialization actually creates new opportunities for outsiders (Epstein, 2019, p. 160). For AI leaders, the implication is uncomfortable: your most advanced technical team may be poorly placed to see the answer to a problem it has studied for years.

Breadth works through transfer, not through ignorance. A study of an open innovation platform found that diversity of scientific interests among solvers predicted problem-solving success, and that solvers often applied approaches from their own specialty to other areas (Tidd & Bessant, 2014, p. 130). The winners were often specialists who crossed a boundary and brought something with them.

The same pattern shows up in scientific publishing. In an analysis of eighteen million scientific papers, the papers that went on to be widely used featured many conventional combinations of prior knowledge plus an injection of unusual combinations (Epstein, 2019, p. 247). The payoff is slow, however. Papers that made new combinations of knowledge were more likely to be ignored on publication, yet fifteen years later, studies that made multiple new combinations were far more likely to be among the top 1 percent of most-cited papers (Epstein, 2019, p. 248). An organization that evaluates people quarterly will systematically undervalue this kind of work.

Structure matters as much as individuals. Creative networks with porous boundaries, where individuals moved easily among teams, produced successful groups, while networks broken into small isolated clusters did not (Epstein, 2019, p. 246). In the physical and social sciences, teams with more far-flung members, including members from different institutions and countries, tended to be more successful (Epstein, 2019, p. 247). Breadth is a property of the network as well as of the person.

# Breadth under uncertainty

Why does distance help? Part of the answer lies in the kind of problem. Facing uncertain environments and wicked problems, breadth of experience is invaluable, while facing kind problems, narrow specialization can be remarkably efficient (Epstein, 2019, p. 190). Expecting hyperspecialists to extend their skill to wicked problems can have disastrous results (Epstein, 2019, p. 190). Most AI product questions, such as which workflow to redesign, which behavior users will adopt, or which risks matter most, are closer to wicked than kind. Feedback arrives slowly, and it is easy to misread.

Forecasting research makes the point concrete. In a long-running study of forecasters, those who stayed devoted to one tradition and reached for formulaic solutions to ill-defined problems were called hedgehogs, while those who drew from an eclectic array of traditions and accepted ambiguity were called foxes (Epstein, 2019, p. 196). The hedgehogs performed especially poorly on long-term predictions within their own domain of expertise, and they actually got worse as they accumulated credentials and experience (Epstein, 2019, p. 196). Surprise separated the two groups: when an outcome took them by surprise, foxes were much more likely to adjust their ideas, while hedgehogs barely budged (Epstein, 2019, p. 205).

This bears directly on how leaders read model outputs, market signals, and failed pilots. The ability to revise a view after a surprise is a form of judgment, and judgment is where human value concentrates as AI absorbs prediction. As AI takes over prediction, humans will focus more on the judgment role alone (Agrawal et al., 2022, p. 95).

> The people best placed to exercise that judgment on unfamiliar problems are the ones who have learned from many domains rather than one.

Teams amplify the effect. Forecasters on small superteams became 50 percent more accurate in their individual predictions on average, and the superteams beat much larger crowds and prediction markets (Epstein, 2019, p. 198). Complex forecasting might seem to call for narrow specialists, but in the face of uncertainty, individual breadth was critical to those superteams (Epstein, 2019, p. 198).

Leadership follows from this. A neuroscientist argued that a leader who solves unknown problems probably has to be a kind of polymath, and a futurist insisted that although specialists can be part of a team, **the team leader needs to bridge multiple disciplines** (Ahmed, 2018, p. 50). The point is not that specialists are dispensable. It is that someone has to connect them.

# What polymaths look like in AI work

Researchers who studied serial innovators found that they tolerate ambiguity, think in systems, hold technical knowledge from peripheral domains, use analogous domains to find inputs for invention, and read more broadly than other technologists (Epstein, 2019, p. 189).

> Versatility, the ability to move between seemingly unrelated domains, is the core competency of the polymathic mind (Ahmed, 2018, p. 181).

Charles Darwin shows how breadth operates in practice. He juggled multiple projects through a network of at least 231 scientific correspondents grouped roughly into thirteen broad themes (Epstein, 2019, p. 189). Darwin did not know everything. He organized access to everything. AI leaders can recognize the pattern: a broad network, organized by themes, feeding a small number of central questions.

AI engineering already rewards this shape. More AI engineers are coming from web development or full-stack backgrounds, and with foundation models, AI engineers tend to be much more involved in building the product than machine learning engineers traditionally were (Huyen, 2025, p. 47).

> The boundaries between model work, product work, and interface work are blurring, and people who can cross them are gaining influence.

The skills organizations need alongside AI also look polymathic. Companies will need to hire for and develop eight new fusion skills:

- **Intelligent interrogation:** knowing how best to ask an AI assistant questions, across levels of abstraction, to get the insights you need.
- **Bot-based empowerment:** people collaborating with intelligent assistants to punch above their weight at work.
- **Reciprocal apprenticing:** teaching AI assistants new skills while also undergoing on-the-job training to work well within AI-enhanced processes.
- **Holistic melding:** developing mental models of AI agents that improve collaborative outcomes.
- **Rehumanizing time:** reimagining business processes to amplify the time available for distinctly human tasks and learning.
- **Responsible normalizing:** shaping the purpose and perception of human-machine collaborations as they relate to individuals, businesses, and society.
- **Judgment integration:** choosing a course of action amid machine uncertainty.
- **Relentless reimagining:** thinking of novel ways to overhaul work, processes, and business models to obtain exponential increases in improvement.

Each of these depends on moving between levels of abstraction, domains, and stakeholders rather than mastering only one.

Execution skill, meanwhile, is becoming easier to share. In studies of software developers, those using generative coding tools completed tasks more quickly than control groups, and the tools appear to narrow the gap between best and worst performers (Daugherty & Wilson, 2024, p. 134).

> When assistance narrows performance gaps in routine execution, differentiation shifts toward framing problems and connecting domains, which is where polymaths work.

# What leaders should do

Start with how you hire. Human resources policies at mature companies often have such specialized slots that potential serial innovators look like round pegs for square holes and get screened out (Epstein, 2019, p. 190). Serial innovators repeatedly claimed that they themselves would be screened out under their company's current hiring practices (Epstein, 2019, p. 190). Researchers advise looking for wide-ranging interests, multiple hobbies, and candidates who focus on the boundaries and interfaces with other systems (Epstein, 2019, p. 190).

> In practice, add interview questions about what candidates have built or studied outside their role, and ask them to describe where their work meets other systems.

Next, defend the interface. Interdisciplinary grant proposals tend to go to reviewers who are highly specialized in one side of the interface, which leaves the interface itself undefended (Epstein, 2019, p. 246). Internal funding and review processes often share the flaw: a proposal that spans two fields is judged by experts in one.

> Put at least one person with cross-field range on every review panel, and give bridging projects a longer evaluation horizon than routine work.

Then send hard problems outward. When an initiative has stalled, ask who is furthest from it, not only who is closest. Pose the problem to teams in unrelated functions or industries, and treat the specialists on the stalled team as the people who will help translate any outside answer into something that works.

Design for movement as well. Given what porous networks do for creative output, rotate people across teams, and avoid leaving the same small group of collaborators together for years.

> Rotation costs short-term efficiency, and that cost is the price of cross-pollination.

Make outside learning a habit. One scientist advises his people to read outside their field every day, because the world becomes bigger and connections become possible (Epstein, 2019, p. 248). Teams can build the same habit by reserving a recurring slot in meetings for something learned outside the field.

Finally, keep the individual in view. Organizations should ensure that the benefits of communal diversity flow both ways, from individuals to the community and back, without glorifying the collective at the expense of the individual (Ahmed, 2018, p. 238). Team structures are necessary, but they are not a substitute for growing and rewarding the versatile people inside them. Give those people career paths that do not require them to pick one specialty.

# The advantage in practice

Specialists remain essential, and kind problems still reward depth. The advantage lies in the combination: **deep expertise on the team, and people with range who connect it to the problems that matter**. As AI makes execution easier to share, that connecting work becomes the scarcer asset. Leaders who recognize it, hire for it, and protect it will be better placed to innovate than those who keep adding depth alone.
