# Summary

- Audit your job slots and screening filters for the people they reject. Ask whether a candidate with several fields of depth and many interests would survive your current process, and if not, change it.
- Add a versatility lane to every hiring rubric, covering breadth of domains worked in, repeated transfer of expertise between fields, and attention to boundaries and interfaces, next to depth in a core area.
- Match the profile to the problem. Staff well-understood, repeatable work with specialists, and put people who have worked across many technologies on the ambiguous, high-uncertainty problems.
- Promote on range of contribution, not only on years in a lane. Experience length alone did not distinguish stronger creators.
- Fund reskilling for business units at the same time as for technical teams, starting with AI champions and managers, and involve domain experts from the first use-case discussion.
- Hire and develop for fusion skills, such as questioning AI well, deciding when the machine is uncertain, and teaching AI systems while learning from them. None of these require machine learning expertise.
- Build career paths that allow lateral moves, rotations, and volunteering skills to other units, so breadth can grow without leaving the company.

Most AI leaders say they want people who can bridge disciplines. Most hiring systems are built to find people who have stayed inside one. If you lead an AI, product, or innovation organization, that mismatch is probably costing you your best inventors before they ever reach an interview.

# Narrow slots screen out serial innovators

Well-defined job slots are efficient for predictable work, but they select against the people who produce the most novel inventions. Researchers who studied serial innovators worried that human resources policies at mature companies have such specialized slots that potential serial innovators look like "round pegs to the square holes" and get screened out (Epstein, 2019, p. 190). Serial innovators themselves repeatedly claimed they would be screened out under their company's current hiring practices (Epstein, 2019, p. 190). The researchers observed that a mechanistic approach to hiring yields highly reproducible results but reduces the number of high-potential innovation candidates (Epstein, 2019, p. 190).

The stigma is also cultural. Polymathy author Waqas Ahmed notes that the pursuit of more than one job, field, or interest has become synonymous with financial suicide, and that a negative stigma attaches to the non-specialist (Ahmed, 2018, p. 131). Success in one field can also pigeonhole a person and make it harder for them to move elsewhere (Ahmed, 2018, p. 266).

Taken together, these pressures mean your funnel can be reliable and still wrong. A rubric that rewards a tidy resume will reproduce the people you already have, and it will quietly discard the candidate whose career looks like a series of detours.

> A reliable hiring process can still be reliably wrong about who your best innovators are.

# What the evidence says about breadth

A study of inventors at 3M found that both specialists and generalists made contributions, and that neither breadth nor depth alone predicted who would win the company's top invention award (Epstein, 2019, p. 182). A third group, labeled polymaths, had depth in a core area plus more breadth than the generalists, having worked across dozens of technology classes, and they were the most likely to succeed in the company and win the award (Epstein, 2019, p. 182). These people repeatedly took expertise from one domain and applied it in a completely new one (Epstein, 2019, p. 182).

> Breadth matters most when the problem is unclear.

An analysis of fifteen years of patents from 32,000 teams found that in low-uncertainty domains, teams of specialists were more likely to author useful patents, while in high-uncertainty domains, teams that included individuals who had worked on a wide variety of technologies were more likely to make a splash (Epstein, 2019, p. 185). The higher the uncertainty, the more important it was to have a high-breadth team member (Epstein, 2019, p. 185).

A study of comic book creators points the same way. Years of experience had no impact on the quality of their work, while the number of genres a creator had worked in did (Epstein, 2019, p. 187). An individual creator who had worked in four or more genres was more innovative than a team whose members collectively had experience across the same number of genres (Epstein, 2019, p. 187).

None of this is an argument against specialists. In kind environments, where the goal is to re-create prior performance with little deviation, teams of specialists work superbly (Epstein, 2019, p. 188). The 3M researcher behind the inventor study reported that specialist contributions peaked around 1985 and then declined, and he hypothesized that **organizations simply need fewer specialists as information becomes broadly available** (Epstein, 2019, p. 183). AI work sits mostly on the uncertain side of that line, where the useful questions are not obvious in advance.

> Specialists win on known problems. Range wins when nobody knows the question yet.

# Recognizing versatility in recruiting and promotion

Versatility is the ability to move seamlessly between various, seemingly unrelated domains (Ahmed, 2018, p. 181). The profile that the serial-innovator researchers called "π-shaped" describes people who dive in and out of multiple specialties (Epstein, 2019, p. 190). Their advice to recruiters was to look for wide-ranging interests, multiple hobbies and avocations, and to notice whether candidates focus on the boundaries and interfaces with other systems when describing their work (Epstein, 2019, p. 190).

Serial innovators share recognizable traits: a high tolerance for ambiguity, systems thinking, the ability to connect disparate pieces of information in new ways, and the habit of reading more, and more broadly, than other technologists (Epstein, 2019, p. 189). A "T-shaped" person has breadth and can go to deep specialists with questions, and one innovator described herself this way (Epstein, 2019, p. 185). The T-shape is a recruiting metaphor popularized by IDEO CEO Tim Brown, in which the vertical bar is deep skill in one area and the horizontal bar is the ability to collaborate and apply knowledge across areas (Bessant et al., 2024, p. 140). The π-shape extends that logic to people with more than one area of depth.

Interviewers can test for this directly. Ask candidates to describe their work and listen for where they linger. Specialists tend to describe the inside of a component. Versatile innovators tend to describe where the component meets everything else.

Promotion systems should look for the same signals. Because experience length did not separate stronger creators from weaker ones, tenure in a single lane is a poor proxy for potential (Epstein, 2019, p. 187). A 3M inventor who worked with more than a hundred different teams in his first eight years said his breadth helped him identify high-impact projects that nobody handed to him (Epstein, 2019, p. 185). Polymaths in modern organizations often survive as generalists whose role is to correct the narrowness of other specialists (Burke, 2020, p. 144). If your promotion ladder only climbs inside one function, you are asking those people to leave.

> Tenure measures time in a lane. It does not measure the ability to cross lanes.

# Reskilling business units alongside technical teams

AI capability does not live only in engineering. CIOs surveyed by Gartner named a lack of necessary staff skills as the most important barrier to AI in their organizations, and the recommended response is a reskilling strategy broad enough that every function and level is part of it (Carmona, 2020, p. 121). That strategy should cover technical departments, business units, and all employees (Carmona, 2020, p. 122).

> Business units need reskilling because otherwise the gap between business and technology teams is too wide for a meaningful dialogue (Carmona, 2020, p. 124).

The depth required is lower than for technical teams: business leaders do not need to learn how to develop AI, but what can be done with it (Carmona, 2020, p. 124). A first attempt to drive an AI transformation in Microsoft's finance organization failed because it did not involve domain experts, while the second attempt trained the entire organization on the possibilities of AI and encouraged it to propose scenarios, which surfaced forecasting as a top pain point (Carmona, 2020, p. 125). Domain experts are also best equipped to detect subtle dataset bias and unintended associations, so credit experts belong in the design of a credit scoring system, and doctors in medical AI models (Carmona, 2020, p. 139).

Collaborators in AI projects often speak different languages and prioritize different outcomes, so shared vocabularies and mutual respect across functions are vital (Runtasewee & González Sánchez, 2026, p. 27). People who can translate between business needs and technical teams close the gaps between executives, domain experts, and engineers (Runtasewee & González Sánchez, 2026, p. 18). A skills assessment should aim to understand gaps across functions and business areas, not to evaluate each employee (Carmona, 2020, p. 126).

This is where versatility becomes an organizational capability, not only a hiring preference. You will never hire enough π-shaped people to cover every business unit, so you must raise the AI literacy of the specialists you already have until they can hold a real conversation with the technical team.

> Train the business to ask better questions of AI, and the technical team will finally have better problems to solve.

# Skills that make people effective with AI

Daugherty and Wilson (2024, pp. 39-40) identify eight fusion skills:

- Intelligent interrogation
- Bot-based empowerment
- Reciprocal apprenticing
- Holistic melding
- Rehumanizing time
- Responsible normalizing
- Judgment integration
- Relentless reimagining

These skills do not require expertise in machine learning or programming, but rather thoughtful humans who are eager to adapt them to the needs of their business (Daugherty & Wilson, 2024, p. 325).

Three of them matter most for hiring conversations. Intelligent interrogation is knowing how best to ask questions of AI across levels of abstraction to get the insights you need (Daugherty & Wilson, 2024, p. 336). Judgment integration is the ability to decide a course of action when a machine is uncertain (Daugherty & Wilson, 2024, p. 333). At Capital One, employees are encouraged to voice concerns when something feels ethically problematic even when a model returns very good accuracy (Daugherty & Wilson, 2024, p. 334). Reciprocal apprenticing means humans and machines continuously learn from each other (Daugherty & Wilson, 2024, p. 345), and **giving people some control over training a system can make them see AI as a colleague rather than a foe** (Daugherty & Wilson, 2024, p. 346).

Learning ability is the underlying trait. One of the most important characteristics for a worker in the age of human-machine hybrids is not necessarily having the exact skill for a job but being able to learn (Daugherty & Wilson, 2024, p. 348). Relentless reimagining, creating new processes and business models rather than simply automating old ones, is the foundation for the other skills (Daugherty & Wilson, 2024, pp. 348, 350). Such relational competence is rarely discussed in corporate talent development programs (Daugherty & Wilson, 2024, p. 351).

Notice how closely these skills resemble the traits of serial innovators. Asking good questions across levels of abstraction is what a person with breadth does naturally, and deciding under uncertainty is what they do daily. The same people who get screened out of narrow slots are often the ones who collaborate best with machines.

> The skill that matters most with AI is not prompting. It is knowing what to ask, and when not to trust the answer.

# Concrete changes to hiring rubrics and career paths

Hiring rubrics:

- Add rubric items for wide-ranging interests, multiple hobbies and avocations, and a focus on the boundaries and interfaces with other systems when candidates describe their work (Epstein, 2019, p. 190).
- Look for evidence that candidates have worked across many domains and carried expertise from one into another, as the most successful 3M polymaths did (Epstein, 2019, p. 182).
- Keep a depth requirement in at least one core area, since the most successful 3M inventors combined depth with breadth (Epstein, 2019, p. 182).
- Add work-style criteria that suit AI organizations: comfort in agile environments, ownership of the end-to-end experience, and collaboration across groups instead of producing individual deliverables in a vacuum (Carmona, 2020, p. 128).
- Probe fusion skills with practical exercises, such as asking candidates to question an AI system across levels of abstraction (Daugherty & Wilson, 2024, p. 336) and to decide what to do when the system is uncertain (Daugherty & Wilson, 2024, p. 333).
- Match the profile to the problem: specialists for kind, repeatable work, and high-breadth people for high-uncertainty work (Epstein, 2019, p. 185).

Career paths:

- Use staff rotation and cross-functional teamworking as routines for removing organizational barriers to learning and innovation (Bessant, 2003, p. 40).
- Let employees volunteer newly acquired skills for projects in other units, even outside their day-to-day job (Bessant et al., 2024, p. 140).
- Treat job rotation as a way to keep people engaged and stimulated, while recognizing that few corporate generalists will become true polymaths (Ahmed, 2018, p. 275).
- Create a recognized generalist track whose role is to connect fields and correct the narrowness of specialists (Burke, 2020, p. 144).
- Fund continuous learning, since leaders will need to invest in the learning capabilities of people who are anxious about their jobs: in one survey, 95 percent of employees saw positive value in working with generative AI, but 60 percent were anxious about their jobs (Daugherty & Wilson, 2024, p. 76).

Start small. Pick one team that owns an ambiguous AI problem, rewrite its job descriptions and interview loop with the changes above, and compare the results with a team hired the old way. Then scale what works.

> If your rubric cannot describe the person who would invent your next product, rewrite the rubric.
