# Summary

- Put people with range in charge of novel, ambiguous AI product decisions. Where patterns do not repeat and feedback is slow or misleading, generalists tend to outperform both seasoned specialists and algorithms.
- Classify every significant decision before you staff it: do patterns repeat, how fast and accurate is feedback, how rich is the data, and how costly is a confident error. Give kind problems to specialists and automation, and give wicked problems to generalists.
- Treat early metrics on novel AI products as provisional, and schedule outcome reviews for when slow feedback finally arrives.
- Require an outside view before approving estimates for new initiatives: a reference class of structurally similar projects from other domains, not one favorite analogy.
- Train people to update their beliefs, and review surprises and failed predictions with the same rigor you give successes.
- Write down where AI decides, where it escalates to a person, and who sets the payoffs, because judgment about what matters stays with people.
- Fund evaluation with domain experts, and do not let your roadmap shrink to what is easy to measure.
- Counter automation bias with independent verification, and diversify a culture that conforms too consistently to its own process.

# Experience helps only where the world is kind

Leaders are told to trust experience, and they are told to trust algorithms. Each piece of advice works in some places and fails in others. The deciding factor is the learning environment.

Kahneman and Klein agreed that whether experience builds expertise depends entirely on the domain (Epstein, 2019, p. 27). In kind environments, patterns repeat over and over, and feedback is extremely accurate and usually very rapid (Epstein, 2019, p. 27). In wicked environments, the rules are often unclear or incomplete, the patterns may not be obvious, and feedback is often delayed, inaccurate, or both (Epstein, 2019, p. 28).

> Experience compounds in kind worlds. In wicked worlds, it can compound the wrong lessons.

Kahneman learned this firsthand. His team of evaluators held regular statistics days, learned each time that their predictions were barely better than blind guessing, and still gained confidence without improving (Epstein, 2019, p. 27). Confidence grew while accuracy did not, which is what a wicked environment does to people who mistake repetition for learning.

# When experience teaches the wrong lesson

A renowned New York physician diagnosed typhoid by feeling patients’ tongues, and his diagnoses kept proving right, but his own hands were in effect spreading the disease (Epstein, 2019, p. 28). His repeated success taught him the worst possible lesson, because it rewarded the very habit that was causing harm (Epstein, 2019, p. 28). Few environments are that wicked, but it takes little to throw experts off course. Expert firefighters facing a skyscraper fire can be deprived of the intuition they built in years of house fires (Epstein, 2019, p. 28).

> Changing rules expose narrow expertise.

When the order of play in bridge was altered, experts had more difficulty adapting than nonexperts, and accountants asked to use a new tax law did worse than novices (Epstein, 2019, p. 37). Repetition can also block discovery. Students rewarded for repeating a single solution to a puzzle almost never discovered the general rule, while every newcomer did (Epstein, 2019, p. 36). Under pressure, experienced professionals tend to regress to what they know best, and the Challenger managers stuck to their usual tools in the face of an unusual challenge (Epstein, 2019, p. 225).

> The more your track record comes from a kind world, the more dangerous it is to apply it to a wicked one.

# Where algorithms win, and where range beats them

Algorithms are the logical endpoint of kind-world expertise. Kasparov said that anything we can codify and pass to computers, they will do better (Epstein, 2019, p. 29). The economics agree: with rich data, machine prediction works well, and fraud detection, medical diagnosis, and bail decisions fall in that sweet spot (Agrawal et al., 2022, p. 72).

The limits are just as instructive. Humans are sometimes extremely good at prediction with little data, and they are good at analogy (Agrawal et al., 2022, p. 73). If something has never happened before, a machine cannot predict it without human judgment supplying a useful analogy (Agrawal et al., 2022, p. 73). The riskiest case is the machine that gives a precise answer that is confidently wrong (Agrawal et al., 2022, p. 74).

Chess shows what range can do against both experience and algorithms. In a freestyle tournament, a duo of amateurs with three ordinary computers beat the best chess supercomputer and teams of grandmasters using computers (Epstein, 2019, p. 30). The winning humans were the best at coaching multiple computers and synthesizing what they returned (Epstein, 2019, p. 30).

> The edge did not go to the deepest specialist or the strongest machine. It went to the person who knew how to direct the machines.

# Why novel AI product decisions are usually wicked

Most of the hard calls in AI product work look more like the second environment than the first. Four features explain why.

**First, feedback is slow.** For tasks with natural labels, the time from serving a prediction to receiving feedback is the feedback loop length (Huyen, 2022, p. 92). For tasks with long feedback loops, such as fraud detection, natural labels might not arrive for weeks or even months (Huyen, 2022, p. 94). A decision about adoption, trust, or workflow change usually has a longer loop than that.

**Second, the ground moves.** The real world is not stationary, and a model that does great at deployment can degrade over time as the data distribution changes (Huyen, 2022, p. 230). A degenerate feedback loop arises when a system's predictions influence the feedback that shapes the next iteration of the model (Huyen, 2022, p. 233). Your own product can distort the signal you use to judge it.

**Third, evaluation is hard.** The open-ended nature of foundation models undermines evaluation against ground truths, because there are so many possible correct responses (Huyen, 2025, p. 115). The more intelligent models become, the harder they are to evaluate (Huyen, 2025, p. 114). Evaluation is among the biggest bottlenecks to AI adoption, and building only what is easy to measure resembles looking for a lost key under the lamppost (Huyen, 2025, p. 161).

**Fourth, history is thin.** In innovation projects, there most likely will be no historical data from which to assign probabilities to uncertainties (Kerzner, 2023, p. 139). Managers make decisions on mergers, innovation, and partnerships without data on similar past events for their firms, so humans use analogies and models in such unusual situations (Agrawal et al., 2022, p. 114). A former Google designer warned that data can become a crutch for every decision, preventing daring design choices (Kerzner, 2023, p. 138).

> If your roadmap rewards only what you can measure, it will quietly favor the kind problems and neglect the wicked ones that matter most.

# Habits of mind and varied experience that improve judgment

If experience alone cannot be trusted here, what can? In wicked domains that lack automatic feedback, experience alone does not improve performance, habits of mind matter more, and they can be developed (Epstein, 2019, p. 204). In forecasting tournaments, an hour of basic training in these habits improved accuracy (Epstein, 2019, p. 204).

**Take the outside view.** One habit is to list separate events that share deep structural similarities with the current problem, rather than focusing only on its internal details (Epstein, 2019, p. 204). The outside view requires ignoring the unique surface features of a project on which you are the expert (Epstein, 2019, p. 101). The stakes are real. Infrastructure projects worldwide go over budget by an average of 28 percent, in part because managers focus on their own project's details and become overly optimistic (Epstein, 2019, p. 103). A consulting team using analogies to other projects judged a £320 million tram estimate a massive underestimate, and the project opened three years late and headed toward £1 billion (Epstein, 2019, p. 103). One analogy is not enough: a full reference class of analogies was far more accurate than the single best analogy (Epstein, 2019, p. 104).

**Make feedback rigorous.** The best forecasters dissected results in search of lessons, especially from bad predictions, which made a wicked environment a little more kind (Epstein, 2019, p. 205). This assumes lessons are cataloged and discoverable, which means someone has to record them and keep them easy to find.

**Seek disconfirming evidence.** The most science-curious people always looked at new evidence whether or not it agreed with their beliefs, while less curious people became more resistant to contrary evidence as they gained knowledge (Epstein, 2019, p. 202). Darwin copied into his notes any fact that ran contrary to a theory he was working on (Epstein, 2019, p. 203).

> Range is not a personality trait. It is a set of habits, and habits can be trained.

**Vary your experience.** Nobel laureates are at least twenty-two times more likely than other scientists to be amateur performers such as actors, dancers, or magicians (Epstein, 2019, p. 38). One organizational researcher suggests varying challenges within a domain drastically to avoid cognitive entrenchment, and a fellow researcher advises insisting on "having one foot outside your world" (Epstein, 2019, p. 37).

**Build in productive disagreement.** Cultural congruence had no influence on any measure of success at 334 institutions of higher education (Epstein, 2019, p. 225). HR managers who conformed to a standard process regardless of results began learning once they were exposed to research favoring dissent (Epstein, 2019, p. 226). The trick is to identify the dominant culture and diversify it by pushing in the opposite direction (Epstein, 2019, p. 227). An organization that conforms to its own process needs more dissent, and one that improvises needs more discipline.

# Who gets which problem

Facing kind problems, narrow specialization can be remarkably efficient, while facing wicked problems, breadth of experience is invaluable (Epstein, 2019, p. 190). Expecting a hyperspecialist to extend a narrow skill to wicked problems can have disastrous results (Epstein, 2019, p. 190). The leadership task is to sort problems honestly, because hyperspecialists are the people most likely to be handed wicked ones.

A product team already divides labor by risk. In an empowered product team, the product manager owns value and viability, the designer owns usability, and the tech lead owns feasibility (Cagan & Jones, 2021, p. 26). Together, these roles cover the full range of product risks, and the team owns the problem and its results (Cagan & Jones, 2021, p. 27). Questions of feasibility often have faster, cleaner feedback and suit deep specialists.

> Questions of value and adoption are slower and murkier, and they are where range matters most.

**Generalists work best when they can reach specialists.** One innovator describes herself as a T-shaped person who goes to deep specialists with fundamental questions and assembles their answers into a narrative (Epstein, 2019, p. 185). A freestyle chess teammate said the winning approach involved an integrated set of skills that in some cases had nothing to do with playing chess (Epstein, 2019, p. 30).

> The best AI product leaders may be the people who ask specialists the right questions, not the people who could have answered them.

# Where human judgment must surround AI

None of this means setting algorithms aside. It means placing people where algorithms are weakest. Prediction is only one component of a decision, and judgment, action, and data are complements that rise in value as prediction becomes cheap (Agrawal et al., 2022, p. 88).

**Start with the payoffs.** Judgment means determining the relative payoff of each possible outcome, including the payoffs of mistakes (Agrawal et al., 2022, p. 94). Programming rewards in advance can automate actions, but the AI may over-optimize one metric and act inconsistently with the organization's broader goals (Agrawal et al., 2022, p. 104).

> When there are too many action-situation combinations to judge in advance, it is more efficient for a human to apply judgment after the prediction (Agrawal et al., 2022, p. 106).

**Next, design the exceptions.** Prediction by exception engages the human only when a scenario is non-routine, which lets one person supervise a prediction machine's routine predictions (Agrawal et al., 2022, p. 81). ZipRecruiter found a price increase raised daily profits by over 50 percent for some new customers (Agrawal et al., 2022, p. 105). But it recognized a longer-term risk: customers paying higher prices might look for alternatives and eventually switch to a competitor (Agrawal et al., 2022, p. 105). So it deliberately waited four months to see whether they would leave, and only then judged the increase safe to implement (Agrawal et al., 2022, p. 105). The profit jump was an immediate signal, but the true test, whether customers would stay, could only be seen over time. Resisting the urge to act on the fast signal is the kind of judgment that algorithms and routine experience do not supply.

**Then, keep evaluation human.** Many teams use human evaluation as the North Star metric, and LinkedIn manually evaluates up to 500 daily conversations with its AI systems (Huyen, 2025, p. 204). Human experts can review a subset of each day’s outputs to detect changes in performance or unusual patterns in usage (Huyen, 2025, p. 204). This matters most in production, where reference data might not be immediately available, but actual users are (Huyen, 2025, p. 204). It also takes real expertise, because judging sophisticated outputs means more than checking how they sound: you may need to fact-check, reason, and bring in domain knowledge (Huyen, 2025, p. 115). A daily expert review is a small, steady way to keep judgment close to a system whose feedback would otherwise arrive too slowly or too vaguely to trust.

**Finally, guard against complacency.** Automation bias can lead people to defer to AI decisions without sufficient oversight (Urbach & Feulner, 2026, p. 257). Experiments on reasoning puzzles show how strong the pull can be: participants who consulted an AI assistant followed its faulty advice on roughly four out of five trials, and their accuracy fell below what they achieved without AI (Shaw & Nave, 2026). Training people to question AI outputs and to verify recommendations independently can reduce blind reliance (Urbach & Feulner, 2026, p. 257). Accountability can also be built in. When participants were rewarded for accuracy and given immediate feedback on each answer, they more than doubled how often they rejected faulty AI advice (Shaw & Nave, 2026). Continuous monitoring is central to accountability, because systems can become inaccurate as the environment or data changes (Carmona, 2020, p. 148).

> Let the algorithm predict. Keep people responsible for deciding what the prediction is worth.

# A practical triage

**Ask four questions of each decision: do patterns repeat, how fast is feedback, how rich is the data, and how costly is a confident error. Then sort.**

- Send decisions with repeating patterns, fast feedback, and rich data to specialists and automation, with monitoring.
- Send decisions with slow or misleading feedback, thin data, or novel structure to generalists, supported by outside-view analysis and rigorous reviews.
- Place a person with judgment wherever AI predictions meet payoffs, exceptions, and evaluation.

Specialists and algorithms remain essential where the world is kind. The advantage in everything else belongs to people with range, and to the leaders who put them in the right seats.
